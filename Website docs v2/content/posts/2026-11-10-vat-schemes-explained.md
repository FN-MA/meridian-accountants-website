**Quick answer:** you must register for VAT once your taxable turnover goes over **£90,000** in any rolling 12 months. After that, the scheme you choose decides when you pay VAT and how much admin you have. The standard scheme suits most businesses that are paid straight away, cash accounting helps if customers pay on credit, and the Flat Rate Scheme can simplify things, but it isn't always cheaper.

VAT is one of the trickiest taxes for small businesses. Even the courts have had to decide whether a Jaffa Cake is a cake or a biscuit. In 1991 a VAT tribunal ruled it's a cake, so it's zero-rated, while a chocolate-covered biscuit is standard-rated at 20%.

This guide gives a few everyday examples from hospitality, then focuses on what matters most for your cash flow: when to register, and which VAT scheme to choose. It uses the rules for 2026/27. The Autumn Budget on 28 October 2026 didn't change the registration threshold or the VAT rate for hospitality.

## Why VAT trips people up: hospitality examples

Most food is zero-rated, but there are plenty of exceptions, and in a café or restaurant the same sandwich can be taxed differently depending on where it's eaten.

| What you sell | VAT rate |
| --- | --- |
| Any food or drink eaten on your premises, hot or cold | 20% |
| Hot takeaway food, such as a hot pasty or fish and chips | 20% |
| Hot takeaway drinks, such as coffee to go | 20% |
| Cold takeaway food, such as a sandwich or salad to go | 0% |
| Ice cream, sweets, chocolate and crisps, eaten in or taken away | 20% |
| Soft drinks and alcohol, eaten in or taken away | 20% |
| Cakes (including Jaffa Cakes) to take away | 0% |

A few points that catch people out:

- **Eating in is always 20%.** It's the eat-in versus takeaway question that matters most, not just whether the food is hot or cold.
- **"Hot" means hotter than the air around it**, and kept or sold hot. A pastry that's been baked and simply left to cool, sold in a paper bag, can stay zero-rated.
- **Service charges** that customers must pay are part of the price and carry 20% VAT. Tips that are freely given are outside VAT.

These rules come from HMRC's guidance on catering and takeaway food (VAT Notice 709/1) and on food (VAT Notice 701/14). If your menu has a mix of eat-in, takeaway, hot and cold, it's worth getting your till set up correctly from day one.

## When you must register for VAT

The registration threshold is **£90,000 of VAT-taxable turnover**. That's your sales of anything that isn't exempt from VAT, including zero-rated sales. There are two tests, and you must register if you meet either:

1. **Looking back:** your taxable turnover for the **last 12 months** goes over £90,000. This is a rolling 12 months, not your accounting year or the tax year. Register within 30 days of the end of the month you went over. Your registration starts from the first day of the second month after that.
2. **Looking ahead:** you expect your taxable turnover to go over £90,000 in the **next 30 days alone**, for example because of a big contract. Register by the end of those 30 days.

**Example:** a café's sales for the 12 months to 31 March 2027 reach £91,500. It must register by 30 April 2027, and it's VAT-registered from 1 May 2027.

**Check every month.** A busy summer can push a seasonal business over the limit without anyone noticing. Register late and HMRC will still want the VAT from the date you should have registered, even though you didn't charge it to customers, plus a penalty.

**Registering before you have to** can make sense for some businesses:

- **If most of your sales are zero-rated**, such as a greengrocer or a farm shop selling unprocessed food. You charge 0% VAT, so prices don't go up, but you can reclaim the 20% VAT on costs such as shop fittings, equipment, vans and repairs. HMRC will usually owe you money each quarter.
- **If your customers are mostly VAT-registered businesses.** They can reclaim the VAT you charge, so it doesn't really cost them, and you can reclaim VAT on your own costs.
- **If you're about to spend a lot on equipment or a fit-out**, registering first lets you reclaim the VAT on it.

It rarely suits businesses selling standard-rated goods to the public, like cafés and restaurants, because your prices go up by 20% or your margins go down.

**Deregistering:** if your taxable turnover falls below **£88,000**, you can ask HMRC to cancel your registration.

## Zero-rated isn't the same as exempt

Both mean you don't add VAT to the price, but they work very differently.

| | Zero-rated | Exempt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| VAT charged to customers | 0% | None, it's outside VAT |
| Counts towards the £90,000 threshold | Yes | No |
| Can you reclaim VAT on related costs? | Yes | No |
| Examples | Most food, including cold takeaway; books and newspapers; children's clothes and shoes; most passenger transport; new homes | Insurance; most financial services; renting out residential property; most education and healthcare; postage stamps |

This matters in practice:

- **A business with only zero-rated sales** can still have to register once those sales go over £90,000, but it can reclaim VAT on its costs, so registering usually puts money back in its pocket. It can also ask HMRC for an exemption from registration if it would rarely owe VAT.
- **A business with only exempt sales**, such as a residential landlord, can't register for VAT on those sales and can't reclaim VAT on its costs. The VAT on its costs is a real cost to the business.
- **A business with a mix** of taxable and exempt sales is "partly exempt", and may only be able to reclaim part of its VAT on costs.

## The VAT schemes explained

Once you're registered, the scheme you use changes **when** you pay VAT and, with the Flat Rate Scheme, **how much**.

### Standard VAT accounting (invoice or accrual basis)

This is the default. You pay VAT on sales based on the date of your invoice or sale, and reclaim VAT on costs based on the date of your supplier's invoice, whether or not money has changed hands yet.

- **Good for:** businesses paid straight away, and businesses that get credit from suppliers, because you can reclaim VAT on purchases before you've paid for them
- **Watch out for:** paying VAT to HMRC on sales your customers haven't paid for yet

### Cash accounting scheme

You pay VAT on sales only when your customer pays you, and reclaim VAT on costs only when you've paid your supplier.

- **Join if:** your VAT-taxable turnover is **£1.35 million or less**
- **Must leave if:** it goes over **£1.6 million**
- **Good for:** businesses that give customers credit terms, such as 30 or 60 days, or that suffer from late payers and bad debts
- **Watch out for:** slower VAT reclaims on your costs, since you can't reclaim until you've paid

### Flat Rate Scheme (FRS)

Instead of working out VAT on each sale and purchase, you pay HMRC a **fixed percentage of your total turnover, including VAT**. You still charge customers 20% as normal, but you generally can't reclaim VAT on your costs.

- **Join if:** you expect your VAT-taxable turnover to be **£150,000 or less** (excluding VAT) in the next 12 months
- **Must leave if:** your total turnover, **including VAT**, goes over **£230,000** in the last 12 months, or you expect it to in the next 30 days alone
- **First year:** 1% off your flat rate in your first year of VAT registration
- **Limited cost businesses** that spend very little on goods (under 2% of turnover, or under £1,000 a year) must use **16.5%**, which often makes the scheme poor value
- **Capital purchases:** you can still reclaim VAT on single capital assets costing over £2,000 including VAT

The percentage depends on your trade. For hospitality:

| Business type | Flat rate |
| --- | --- |
| Catering, including restaurants and takeaways | 12.5% |
| Hotel or accommodation | 10.5% |

The flat rate applies to **all** your turnover, including zero-rated sales such as cold takeaway food. If a large share of your sales are zero-rated, the FRS can cost more than the standard scheme. Always compare the two before joining.

### Annual accounting scheme

You file **one VAT return a year** instead of four, and pay in instalments during the year. Available if your VAT-taxable turnover is **£1.35 million or less**. It can be combined with cash accounting or the Flat Rate Scheme.

### At a glance

| Scheme | VAT on sales paid | VAT on costs reclaimed | Turnover limit to join |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Standard | When you invoice or sell | When your supplier invoices | No limit |
| Cash accounting | When the customer pays | When you pay the supplier | £1.35m |
| Flat Rate Scheme | Fixed % of total turnover | Generally none (except capital items over £2,000) | £150,000 |
| Annual accounting | Instalments, one return a year | As standard or cash | £1.35m |

## Choosing a scheme: start with how you're paid

The best scheme depends largely on **when your customers pay you** and **when you pay your suppliers**.

- **Customers pay straight away** (cafés, restaurants, hotels, shops): cash accounting gives you no advantage on sales, because the cash arrives when you make the sale. If your suppliers give you credit, the **standard scheme** lets you reclaim VAT on purchases from the invoice date, before you've paid. For most hospitality businesses, the choice is between the standard scheme and the Flat Rate Scheme.
- **Customers pay on credit terms** (trades, consultants, wholesalers, anyone invoicing on 30 or 60 days): **cash accounting** means you don't pay HMRC VAT you haven't collected yet, and you get automatic relief if a customer never pays.
- **Few costs with VAT on them** (many service businesses): the **Flat Rate Scheme** can save time and sometimes money, unless the 16.5% limited cost rate applies.

## Worked example: is the Flat Rate Scheme worth it?

Two hospitality businesses, each with £100,000 of sales before VAT and about **£15,000 a year of costs with VAT on them** (equipment, cleaning, utilities, packaging), so **£3,000** of VAT to reclaim. Most food ingredients are zero-rated, so they don't add to this.

| | Café (mostly eat-in) | Sandwich shop (half cold takeaway) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard-rated sales (excl. VAT) | £100,000 | £50,000 |
| Zero-rated sales | £0 | £50,000 |
| Total turnover including VAT | £120,000 | £110,000 |
| **Standard scheme:** VAT on sales less VAT on costs | £20,000 − £3,000 = **£17,000** | £10,000 − £3,000 = **£7,000** |
| **Flat Rate Scheme:** 12.5% of total turnover | **£15,000** | **£13,750** |
| Better option | FRS saves about £2,000 | Standard saves about £6,750 |

The café is better off on the Flat Rate Scheme, and saves even more in its first year with the 1% discount. The sandwich shop would **overpay by about £6,750 a year** on the Flat Rate Scheme, because the flat rate is charged on its zero-rated sales too.

The figures are for illustration only. Your own result depends on your mix of sales and costs, so it's worth running the numbers each year, and again whenever your menu or turnover changes.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the VAT registration threshold for 2026/27?

£90,000 of VAT-taxable turnover in any rolling 12-month period, or if you expect to go over £90,000 in the next 30 days alone. The Autumn Budget on 28 October 2026 didn't change it.

### Is food VAT-free?

Most food sold to take away cold is zero-rated, but food and drink eaten on your premises, hot takeaway food and drink, and items such as ice cream, sweets, crisps, soft drinks and alcohol are standard-rated at 20%.

### What's the difference between zero-rated and exempt?

Zero-rated sales are taxable at 0%: they count towards the £90,000 threshold and you can reclaim VAT on related costs. Exempt sales, such as insurance or residential rent, are outside VAT: they don't count towards the threshold and you can't reclaim VAT on related costs.

### Should I register for VAT before I reach £90,000?

It can make sense if most of your sales are zero-rated, like a greengrocer, because you can reclaim VAT on your costs without raising your prices. It can also suit businesses whose customers are mostly VAT-registered. It rarely suits businesses selling standard-rated goods to the public.

### When do I have to leave the Flat Rate Scheme?

When your total turnover including VAT goes over £230,000 in the last 12 months, or you expect it to go over £230,000 in the next 30 days alone.

### Is the Flat Rate Scheme always cheaper?

No. You pay a fixed percentage of all your turnover, including zero-rated sales, and you can't reclaim VAT on most costs. Businesses with lots of zero-rated sales, or very low costs (which must use 16.5%), can end up paying more VAT on the Flat Rate Scheme than they would on the standard scheme.

### Should a restaurant use cash accounting?

Usually there's little benefit, because customers pay at the time of sale. Restaurants and cafés that get credit from suppliers are often better on the standard scheme, which lets them reclaim VAT on purchases from the invoice date.

### Can I change VAT scheme?

Yes. You can usually join or leave a scheme at the start of a VAT period, as long as you meet its conditions. After leaving the Flat Rate Scheme you must wait 12 months before rejoining.

## In short

- Register once your taxable turnover goes over £90,000 in any rolling 12 months, and check every month.
- Choose your scheme based on how you're paid: cash accounting for credit terms, standard or Flat Rate for businesses paid straight away.
- The Flat Rate Scheme isn't always cheaper, especially with lots of zero-rated sales. Run the numbers each year.
