**Quick answer:** as an employer, you can give your staff small gifts tax-free, with no National Insurance or paperwork, as long as each gift costs **£50 or less**, isn't cash or a cash voucher, isn't a reward for their work, and isn't something they're entitled to under their contract. They can be given at any time of year: Christmas, Easter, Eid, Diwali, a birthday, or just as a one-off thank you. Directors of small family companies can receive these gifts too, up to **six £50 gifts a year** (£300 in total).

These are called **trivial benefits**, and they're one of the simplest tax-free ways to treat your team. With the festive season coming up, this guide explains what counts, what doesn't, and how it works alongside the tax-free Christmas party allowance.

It's written for **employers and business owners**, including directors of their own limited companies.

## What counts as a trivial benefit?

A gift to an employee is a trivial benefit, and completely tax-free, if it meets **all four** of these conditions:

| Condition | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **It costs £50 or less** | The cost to you per employee, including VAT and delivery. |
| **It isn't cash or a cash voucher** | A bottle of wine or a gift card for a shop is fine. Cash, a bonus or a voucher that can be swapped for cash isn't. |
| **It isn't a reward for work or performance** | A Christmas gift to everyone is fine. A gift for hitting a target or as thanks for working late isn't. |
| **It isn't in their contract** | It can't be something the employee is entitled to, or part of a salary sacrifice. |

If a gift meets all four, there's **no income tax, no National Insurance, nothing to report on a P11D or through payroll**, and no need to ask HMRC. The cost is also normally an allowable expense for your business.

**The £50 limit is a cliff edge, not an allowance.** If a gift costs £55, the whole £55 is taxable, not just the £5 over. Keep each gift at or under £50.

There's no limit on how many trivial benefits an employee can receive in a year, as long as each one meets the conditions. Directors of small family companies are the exception (see below).

## Christmas gifts: what qualifies and what doesn't

| Usually qualifies (£50 or less each) | Doesn't qualify |
| --- | --- |
| A bottle of wine, chocolates or a festive hamper | Cash, or a Christmas bonus paid through payroll |
| A turkey or a box of food for Christmas dinner | A voucher that can be exchanged for cash |
| A gift card for a shop, restaurant or cinema | A gift for hitting a target or as thanks for extra work |
| Flowers or a small gift for a birthday or new baby | A £60 hamper (the whole £60 becomes taxable) |
| A team lunch or takeaway that isn't a reward for work | A gift promised in the employee's contract |

**Gift cards are fine, cash isn't.** A £50 gift card for a shop or restaurant is a non-cash voucher and can qualify. Putting £50 in an envelope or adding it to their pay can't.

**Keep the message right.** Give the gift because it's Christmas, not because of how someone has performed. A card saying "Merry Christmas from all of us" is safe. "Thanks for hitting your sales target" turns the same gift into taxable pay.

**Gifts for a whole team.** If you buy something shared, like a hamper for the office, and it's impractical to work out each person's share, you can divide the total cost by the number of people. Each person's share must be £50 or less.

**Gifts through the year count too.** Trivial benefits aren't just for Christmas. Birthdays, a new baby, a wedding or a get-well-soon gift can all qualify, as long as each one meets the four conditions.

## Directors of small companies: the £300 a year cap

If you're a director of a **close company** (broadly, a company controlled by 5 or fewer people, which covers most family and owner-managed companies), you can still receive trivial benefits, but there's an annual cap of **£300 per tax year** in total. Gifts to family members count too, in different ways depending on whether they work for the company (see below).

**The £300 isn't a single £300 gift.** Each gift must still be **£50 or less**, and the £300 is the most all of them can add up to in a tax year. In practice, that's **up to six £50 gifts a year**, for example at Christmas, Easter or Eid, on your birthday and a couple of other occasions, or more gifts if they cost less. A single gift of £300, or even £60, doesn't qualify.

Employees who aren't directors don't have this £300 cap.

### Example: what the £300 is worth

The company pays for £300 of gifts for its director in the tax year. Compare that with paying the same £300 as a dividend:

|  | Basic-rate director | Higher-rate director |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profit needed to pay a £300 dividend after corporation tax (19%) and dividend tax | £415 | £576 |
| Profit needed to give £300 of trivial benefits | £300 | £300 |
| **Saving** | **£115** | **£276** |

This assumes the director has already used their £500 dividend allowance. It isn't a large sum, but it's a simple saving that many directors miss. Two directors can each have their own £300.

**Keep it genuine.** The gifts should be real gifts on real occasions, not a regular monthly payment in disguise. Paying for the director's weekly shop with a gift card every month isn't what the relief is for, and HMRC may challenge it.

### What about family members?

The £300 cap also reaches a director's family and household, such as a spouse or partner, children and parents. How it works depends on whether the family member works for the company.

| Family member | Each gift £50 or less? | Which £300 cap it uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Employed by the company** (but not a director) | Yes | **Their own £300**, separate from the director's |
| **Not employed by the company** | Yes | **The director's £300** |

**Why does an employed family member have a cap at all?** Ordinary employees can receive any number of £50 gifts a year. But in a family company, the owners decide who gets gifts, so the cap stops a director routing unlimited tax-free gifts to relatives on the payroll. A family member who genuinely works for the company gets their own £300, but not the unlimited amount other staff get.

**The two limits work differently.** The £50 limit applies to **each gift to each person** on its own. The £300 is a **running total** for the tax year. So two £30 gifts to two people are two separate gifts, each under £50, even though together they cost £60.

#### HMRC's examples, explained

**Example 1: a family member who's an employee**

*HMRC's example:* a director receives a £20 bottle of wine, and the director's spouse, who is an employee of the company, also receives a £20 bottle. £20 is allocated against each of their annual exempt amounts.

*What this means:* each bottle is a separate gift under £50, so both qualify. Because the spouse is an employee, their bottle uses **their own** £300, not the director's. Afterwards, the director has £280 left and the spouse has £280 left.

**Example 2: a family member who isn't an employee**

*HMRC's example:* a director and the director's daughter each receive a £30 turkey. The daughter isn't an employee or office holder of the company, so the total of £60 counts against the director's annual exempt amount.

*What this means:* each turkey is a separate gift under £50, so both qualify. But because the daughter doesn't work for the company, her turkey is treated as a gift to the director. Both go on the director's running total: £30 + £30 = £60 used, £240 left.

**Example 3: two directors and a child who isn't an employee**

*HMRC's example:* a married couple are both directors, and they and their son each receive a £30 gift token. The son isn't an employee or office holder, so the cost of his token is split equally between his parents. Each director is charged £45 against their annual exempt amount.

*What this means:* each director uses £30 for their own token plus half the son's £30 (£15), so £45 each. Each director has £255 left.

**What wouldn't qualify:** a single £60 hamper for the whole family. That's one gift costing over £50, so the whole £60 is taxable.

**The job must be real.** A family member's own £300 cap only applies if they genuinely work for the company, with pay that fits the work they do.

## Staff parties and annual events: a separate £150 allowance

Staff parties and celebrations aren't trivial benefits. They have their own exemption, sometimes called the **annual function exemption**, which means you can **give staff a gift and hold an event**, and both can be tax-free.

It isn't just for Christmas. It covers any **annual** staff event, such as a Christmas party, a summer barbecue, an Eid or Diwali meal, a new year celebration or an annual team dinner. An event is tax-free for your staff if:

- the total cost of all your annual events is **£150 or less per person attending** for the tax year, including VAT, food, drink, transport and any overnight stay
- it's **open to all your employees**, or all employees at one site
- it's an **annual event** that happens each year, not a one-off

To work out the cost per head, divide the total cost of each event by everyone who attends, including partners and guests. If an employee brings a partner, the cost for both counts towards that employee's £150.

| Example | Cost per head | Tax-free? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Christmas meal for 8 staff costing £960 | £120 | Yes |
| Eid or Diwali meal for 8 staff costing £1,280 | £160 | No: the whole £160 is taxable, not just £10 |
| Summer barbecue at £40 a head, then a £100 a head Christmas party | £140 in total | Yes, both are covered |
| Summer barbecue at £60 a head, then a £120 a head Christmas party | £180 in total | Only one: choose the Christmas party (£120) as exempt, and the barbecue becomes taxable |
| A one-off pizza lunch for the team | £15 | Not covered by the £150 exemption (it isn't annual), but tax-free as a trivial benefit |
| A one-off dinner for the team at a restaurant | £80 | No: it isn't annual, and at over £50 a head it isn't a trivial benefit either |

**One-off events.** The £150 exemption only covers events you hold every year. A one-off event, like a pizza lunch or drinks to mark an office move, can still be tax-free as a **trivial benefit** if it costs **£50 a head or less** and isn't a reward for work. It's one-off events costing more than £50 a head that become taxable for staff. Either way, the cost is normally still an allowable expense for the business.

If your events add up to more than £150 a head, you can choose which ones the exemption covers, as long as they fit within £150 together.

Like trivial benefits, **£150 is a limit, not an allowance**. Go over it by £1 for an event and the whole cost of that event becomes taxable. If that happens, you can settle the tax for your staff through a **PAYE Settlement Agreement** so they aren't hit with a tax bill.

A company whose only employee is the director can still use the exemption, for example for an annual meal for the director and their partner.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Going just over £50.** Check the cost including VAT and delivery. A £45 hamper with £7 delivery is £52, and fully taxable.
- **Giving cash or adding it to pay.** Even £20 in a Christmas card is taxable pay and must go through payroll.
- **Linking the gift to performance.** Keep Christmas gifts the same for everyone, or at least not tied to targets or extra hours.
- **Directors going over £300.** Keep a running total for each director across the tax year (6 April to 5 April).
- **Regular gifts that look like pay.** A gift card every month to the same person can look like salary, not a trivial benefit.

## Keep simple records

You don't report trivial benefits to HMRC, but keep a note of:

- what you gave, to whom and when
- what it cost, with the receipt
- the occasion, such as Christmas or a birthday

This makes it easy to show each gift met the rules if HMRC ever asks, and to track directors' £300 limits.

**Gifts to clients are different.** The trivial benefits rules only cover gifts to employees and directors. Gifts to clients and customers have their own tax and VAT rules, and food and drink usually aren't tax-deductible as client gifts.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a trivial benefit?

A small gift to an employee that costs £50 or less, isn't cash or a cash voucher, isn't a reward for their work and isn't part of their contract. It's free of income tax and National Insurance, and doesn't need to be reported to HMRC.

### Can I give my staff a gift card at Christmas tax-free?

Yes, if it costs £50 or less and can't be exchanged for cash. A gift card for a shop, restaurant or cinema can qualify. Cash, or a cash bonus through payroll, can't.

### How much can a director receive in trivial benefits?

Directors of close companies, which includes most small owner-managed companies, can receive trivial benefits up to £300 in total per tax year. Each gift must still be £50 or less, so that's up to six £50 gifts a year, not one £300 gift.

### Can I give staff a gift and pay for a staff party?

Yes. They're separate exemptions. A gift of up to £50 can be a trivial benefit, and annual events such as a Christmas party, summer barbecue or Eid meal costing up to £150 per head in total can be tax-free under the annual function exemption.

### Do trivial benefits need to go through payroll from April 2027?

No. The new rules for reporting benefits through payroll from April 2027 cover taxable benefits like company cars. Trivial benefits are exempt, so there's nothing to report. ## In short

- Gifts to staff of £50 or less each can be tax-free, as long as they aren't cash, a reward for work or part of their contract.
- Directors of small companies are limited to £300 a year of these gifts.
- Annual staff events, like a Christmas party or summer barbecue, have a separate £150 per head allowance, so you can do both.
- Go over either limit and the whole amount becomes taxable.
